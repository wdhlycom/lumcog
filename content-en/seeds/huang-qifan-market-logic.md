Among the officials who have run Chinese economic policy, Huang Qifan is an oddity. He was mayor of Chongqing, later sat on the Financial and Economic Affairs Committee of the National People's Congress, and the books he published after stepping down — *Structural Reform*, *Analysis and Reflection* — are still being passed around.

What is most interesting about him is not what he argues for, but how he argues. His vocabulary is careful, almost every phrase inside the approved set. Take the phrases apart, though, and they usually land on the same sentence: let the market do this one.

That mismatch is worth a look. If an argument is sound, why does it need a second language before it can be said out loud?

## The policy pendulum

Local economies fall into the same loop again and again: loosen and it turns to chaos, tighten and it dies. Land finance, property controls, industrial support — the crashes on those three roads are countable.

His answer is a ***long-term mechanism***: land regulation, legal constraints, tax adjustment, a rental market — a set of institutions rather than the short-term tools of purchase limits, lending limits, price caps. Short-term tools work fast, but they require you to stay in the room forever: restrict today, release tomorrow, tighten the day after. The policy itself becomes the largest uncertainty in the market.

When it comes to numbers, he is concrete. Floor land price should not exceed one third of the current apartment price: if units nearby sell at 10,000 yuan a square metre, bidding should stop around 3,300 — beyond that, it is no longer the market pushing prices up, it is people. Total land supply in a city should be capped at about 100 square metres per resident; of that 100, more than 50 should go to roads, utilities, parks, schools and hospitals, industrial land should stay under 20, and each square kilometre should produce 10 billion yuan of output.

What gives those numbers weight is that they can be checked. Anyone can say "promote the steady and healthy development of the real estate market" and mean nothing by it. "Land price within one third of housing price" can be written into a rule and handed to someone to verify. That is the difference.

## Nine parts debt, one part equity

Finance is where he spent most of his attention, and where the irritation is easiest to hear. He cited a figure: in 2017, debt financing accounted for more than ninety percent of all new financing in the economy, equity for less than ten. Over the same period, seventy percent of American corporate financing came from equity and thirty from debt — the mirror image.

Leave that structure alone and the consequence needs no argument, only arithmetic. Corporate leverage sits at seventy percent; if every year's new financing is nine parts debt and one part equity, a few years later it is not seventy, it is eighty or ninety. That is not a moral problem. It is a sum.

So he argues for raising the share of ***equity financing*** toward half of all new financing, with registration-based listing, delisting rules and long-term institutional money as the supporting parts. Every link in that chain is hard, but the difficulty is in execution, not in the reasoning.

On opening up, he is blunter than most. He does not argue for a high wall; he argues for letting foreign institutions in deep enough that interests become entangled — "you inside me, me inside you." It sounds like diplomatic language. What it describes is risk sharing: binding other people's money and other people's reputations into your system is cheaper than any wall.

Would you rather owe someone money, or let them take a share of your decisions? Both cost you. They just cost you in different places.

## An account nobody runs

On tax, he leans toward lighter rates for skilled workers and for firms, on the grounds that if people don't come and capital leaves, the cake never grows and distribution is an argument about nothing. The logic is not new. What is new is his willingness to put the numbers on the table.

The pension case is sharper. In 2018, China's enterprise annuities held 1.47 trillion yuan, 1.6 percent of GDP. America's 401(k) plans held 5.2 trillion dollars, roughly a quarter of GDP. A gap of more than ten times.

In 2020 he proposed something blunter still: convert the 14.6 trillion yuan housing fund into enterprise annuities, with the employer's six percent carried over and the employee's six percent made voluntary.

What he cares about is not only retirement. Long-term money entering the market props up old age at one end and feeds the capital market at the other — ***wealth held by households*** and a deeper capital market are, for him, two faces of one thing.

Everyone worries about retirement. Worry is free; running the numbers takes effort.

When did you last sit down and calculate what you will actually receive each month after you stop working?

## Subsidies don't buy an industry

Toward the fashionable phrases — new productive forces, advanced manufacturing — his attitude is sober. Push innovation through administrative mobilisation, government-picked projects and indiscriminate subsidies, and the money never passes through a market filter; who ends up with it is hard to say afterwards.

When Chongqing wanted the computer industry, he went to Hewlett-Packard. The vice president, having seen a great many local governments, opened with the obvious question: how much subsidy are you offering? None, he said. The deal that followed rested on moving the assembly plant and its component suppliers in together, so that logistics and matching costs fell — not a low price bought with fiscal money, but a low price produced by the model.

He later coined a name for the alternative: ***self-harming investment attraction*** — cut-rate land, cut-rate tax, poaching from the next province, until the firm you courted is courted away by someone cheaper and nobody is left holding anything. Unflattering. Accurate.

Which brings the judgement down to one line: real innovation has to be decided by the people who pay for failure.

The industry your region decided to champion — is it still there?

Looking back over years of speeches and essays, people often reach for vivid political rhetoric to describe how he talks. Strip that away and what remains is plain: he trusts the market, trusts rules more than people, trusts structural change more than campaigns.

He will also date. The land arithmetic was built for a population still growing; a hundred square metres per resident and twenty for industry are rulers cut for that era, and whether they still fit a city that is shrinking is another question. Treating a man as the standard answer is precisely what he argued against.

The core of his position, put plainly: the government should set the rules, not do the market's job for it. That sentence now appears in documents and speeches often enough that it has lost its edge.

The next time you quote one of his numbers, will you still remember the conditions it was calculated under?
