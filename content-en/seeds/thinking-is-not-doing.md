Let me get one thing straight: this is not necessarily you having "achieved nothing." You are more likely caught in a fairly common cognitive trap — ***turning "the search for perfect certainty" into "the precondition for action."*** You think clearly and you have real insight into business and technology. But the smarter you are, the more rules you understand, the more likely you are to run a *full-path simulation* inside your head: you raise an idea, walk it through, spot compliance risk, low willingness to pay, free competitors — and conclude "this road is closed," then drop it. The logic is flawless, and it really does save you the cost of spending months building a business that was doomed anyway. But it leaves an illusion behind: that you've been busy for nothing again.

The simulation is airtight; the thing itself doesn't move an inch. So when was the last time you reasoned an idea into having no flaws at all — and did it ever take a single step afterward?

## Why discussions so rarely turn into action

**First, the logical closed loop of "rational reasoning."** Every step you push it further, you are often also handing yourself one more reason not to move. The premise is right, the inference is sound, but it's a closed loop — it circles back to "don't do it." Have you ever had this: you tore an idea down three times on paper, and it still hadn't written its first line of code?

**Then there's "discussion" itself.** The conversation beforehand is not essentially "preparation for action" — it is often the most efficient form of *risk-checking*. If an idea is full of holes on paper, in logic, it would only die faster in reality. So discovering "it won't work" isn't the discussion failing you; it's the discussion helping you eliminate a wrong option.

The trouble is, you can easily read that "elimination" as "I've achieved nothing" — and that's where the gap comes from. If you've also been treating discussion as the preparation before you start, have you ever counted how many months ago your last real move was?

## Knowing it won't work, why keep talking it through

Have you ever asked yourself this: knowing it most likely won't work, why keep talking it through, again and again? It may be that your brain is meeting two needs in the cheapest possible way.

**One is a sense of safety (avoiding harm).** In reality, starting a company, putting in money, writing code — the cost is enormous. But reasoning on paper carries no financial or legal risk. **The other is a sense of control (seeking gain).** You probably hope to find a perfect plan that is "absolutely workable, risk-free, hugely profitable, and just happens to fit me" — and only then begin.

But the logic of the real world is: there is *almost no* "perfectly workable plan produced by discussion." Actions that genuinely land rarely begin because you've "thought it all the way through"; more often they begin by *running with errors, and correcting through collision.*

In discussion, we never lost a single cent. So in the past six months, which was the one thing you talked about most, and never moved on?

## What actually works

Where you get stuck is mostly confusing "strategic reasoning" with "granularity of execution." Your model right now is probably: I want to build an app → turn it into a product → sell it to retail traders → hit compliance and payment problems → scrap the plan. That granularity is too large; any large plan is fragile in reality.

- **Wrong granularity of action:** "I'll turn it into an A-share quantitative trading system." — too heavy, simply can't move.
- **Right granularity of action:** "Tonight I'll just pull the project locally, run its first demo with `pip install`, and see what the web page looks like." — concrete, finishable in half an hour.

**Treat "testing a hypothesis" as the goal**, not "building a big business." Don't ask upfront "can this help me make a lot of money"; ask "which tiny experiment can I finish with it today." For example, use its research-report feature to generate the prettiest options-analysis chart of Nvidia (NVDA), post it to WeChat Moments or Xiaohongshu, and see if a single person likes it or DMs you. If nobody reacts, your loss is zero, but you've tested one hypothesis; if even one person asks "what software made this," you've got your first positive feedback.

**Finally, tolerate "imperfection," allow "running with errors."** In the military there's a concept called the **OODA loop** (observe, orient, decide, act), from U.S. Air Force Colonel John Boyd. What often decides success is not how perfect the "decision" is, but how long the "action" gets stuck. A rough, flawed move, the moment it's projected into reality — publishing an article, demoing to someone, running one line of code — the data it brings back is often worth more than a hundred perfect, high-definition simulations.
